Module 1 / Foundations

Crawl, render, index: the engineering version

Most SEOs know "Google crawls and ranks." The senior version is a pipeline with queues and budgets, where problems at each stage look completely different.

Why it paysSaaS sites run on React, Next.js, and static site generators. The person who knows whether content exists in the initial HTML or only after JavaScript execution is the person who prevents six figure indexing failures.
Plain EnglishGoogle reads a page twice. First it reads the raw HTML the server sends: think of it as the letter that arrives in the mail. Later, sometimes days later, it runs the page's JavaScript to see the finished version: the letter after someone assembled the furniture inside. WordPress sites (your world) put everything in the letter. Modern SaaS sites often mail an empty box with assembly instructions, and content that only exists after assembly waits in Google's slow lane. The four strategies below are four answers to one question: who assembles the furniture, and when.

The four rendering strategies

StrategyWhat the server sendsSEO risk
SSRFull HTML built per requestLow. Google sees everything on fetch
SSGHTML prebuilt at deploy timeLow. What most SaaS docs use
CSREmpty shell, browser JS builds the pageHigh. Content waits in the render queue
HydrationHTML first, JS wakes it upDepends: what exists before hydration?

The three tests, in order

1. View source, not inspect element

View source shows the raw HTML the server sent. Inspect element shows the DOM after JS ran. If your content is in inspect but not in source, it is JavaScript dependent and at risk.

2. Curl as Googlebot

# does the first fetch contain your content?
curl -A "Googlebot" https://example.com/page | grep "target phrase"

No match means Google's first pass sees nothing. The page depends on the render queue, which can delay indexing by days.

3. URL Inspection in Search Console

Fetch the live URL, read the rendered HTML tab and screenshot. This is what Google's renderer actually produced, including blocked resources.

Checkpoint

0 / 3
A React page shows its pricing table in inspect element but not in view source. What does that mean?
Google CAN render JS, but rendering is a second, delayed pass. Content missing from source is content at risk, not content that is guaranteed dead.
Which setup is the safest for SEO out of the box?
SSG serves complete prebuilt HTML. Nothing waits on JavaScript execution, so nothing waits in the render queue.
Why does the render queue matter commercially?
Rendering is a separate queue with its own delays. For time sensitive launches, JS dependent content means lost days.
Module 2 / Foundations

Log files: the ground truth

Search Console shows you a summary. Server logs show you every single request Googlebot ever made. Reading them is the most respected skill in the posting.

Why it paysBig SaaS sites have hundreds of thousands of URLs and a finite crawl budget. If 60% of Googlebot's visits burn on junk, new product pages index slowly and rank slowly. Finding the waste is the job.
Plain EnglishYour web server keeps a diary: one line for every visit it has ever received, including every single visit from Google's crawler. Search Console shows you a trimmed summary of that diary; the log file is the full security camera footage. Reading it answers one question: of the limited attention Google gives your site each day (the "crawl budget"), how much lands on pages that matter and how much is wasted on junk? Everything in this module is that one question, asked five ways.

Anatomy of a log line

The five standard questions

Where does Google spend?

Most crawled URLs. Money pages or junk parameters?

How much is wasted?

Percent of crawls hitting 3xx, 4xx, 5xx instead of 200.

What shouldn't exist?

Parameter URLs, staging leaks, dead paths still being hit.

How fast is first crawl?

Days between publish and Googlebot's first visit. Your speed to index.

What never gets crawled?

Sections Googlebot ignores are orphaned or deprioritized. Fix with internal links.

The one liner that starts every analysis

# top 50 URL + status combos Googlebot hit
grep "Googlebot" access.log | awk '{print $7, $9}' | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -50

Then verify IPs against Google's published ranges. Anyone can fake the user agent string.

0 / 3
Logs show Googlebot hitting a 301 URL 4,000 times a month. The right fix is:
The redirect isn't the disease, it's the symptom. Something keeps sending Google there. Fix the source links; blocking it strands the signals.
Why verify the IP address on Googlebot log entries?
User agents are self reported. Verified IP ranges (or reverse DNS to googlebot.com) separate real crawl data from scraper noise.
What do logs give you that Search Console cannot?
GSC samples and aggregates. Logs are the complete record, which is why "log file analysis" appears by name in senior postings.
Module 3 / Infrastructure

The implementation layer

Sitemaps, JSON-LD, canonicals, redirects, robots.txt, migrations. You know the concepts. The senior version is knowing the failure modes cold.

Why it paysSaaS companies replatform constantly: marketing site to Next.js, docs to Mintlify. Migrations are the highest stakes event in SEO, and migration expertise is in every senior posting for that reason.
Plain EnglishThis is the module closest to what you already do: redirects, canonicals, and sitemaps are publisher SEO bread and butter. The senior difference is knowing the traps by heart. Four of them: robots.txt cannot remove a page from Google's index, a canonical tag is a suggestion Google may overrule, a sitemap full of broken URLs stops being believed, and a migration that redirects everything to the homepage burns the site's accumulated history. Learn those four cold and you interview at senior level on this topic.

Redirect chains burn budget and dilute signals

Failure modes seniors get tested on

The robots.txt trap

Blocking a URL in robots.txt does NOT deindex it. It prevents Google from crawling, which means Google never sees the noindex tag, so the URL can stay indexed as a bare link. To deindex: allow crawl, serve noindex, wait, then optionally block.

Contradictory canonicals

Canonical pointing at a redirect, or at a noindexed page, sends mixed signals and Google picks for you. Canonical is a hint, not a directive: your internal links and sitemaps must agree with it or it gets ignored.

Sitemap trust decay

Sitemaps full of redirects, 404s, and noindexed URLs teach Google your sitemap lies, and it starts ignoring it. Only canonical, indexable, 200 status URLs go in. Fake lastmod dates get ignored too.

The homepage dump migration

Mass redirecting old URLs to the homepage gets treated as a soft 404 and the equity evaporates. Migrations need a one to one redirect map, kept under version control, executed with canonicals, sitemaps, and internal links updated in the same deploy.

Checkpoint

0 / 3
You need a page OUT of the index. First move:
Google has to crawl the page to see the noindex. Block first and the noindex is invisible, so the URL lingers in the index.
A canonical tag points to a URL that 301s somewhere else. Result?
Canonical is a hint. Point it at anything other than a live 200 canonical URL and you invite Google to override you.
During a domain migration, redirecting every old URL to the new homepage causes:
Google treats irrelevant mass redirects like soft 404s. Old page signals only transfer to genuinely equivalent destinations.
Module 4 / Infrastructure

HTTP, CDN, DNS, and the Vitals

The layer under the website. Speaking this language correctly is how SEOs earn respect from engineering teams.

Why it paysThe classic outage: someone enables aggressive bot protection on Cloudflare, Googlebot gets blocked, rankings crater in days, and nobody in marketing knows why. The SEO Lead is the person who checks firewall events first.
Plain EnglishThe plumbing underneath every website, translated. DNS is the phone book that turns a domain name into a server's address. A CDN like Cloudflare keeps copies of your site around the world so pages load fast, and it employs a bouncer (bot protection) that sometimes throws Googlebot out by mistake: the classic invisible SEO disaster. Status codes are the three digit answers a server gives every request: 200 means fine, 301 means moved permanently, 404 means gone, 500 means broken. Core Web Vitals are Google's three stopwatches measuring how fast and stable a page feels to real visitors.

Status codes that actually matter

304Not modifiedGood. Caching works, crawl budget saved
410GoneDeindexes slightly faster than 404 when killing content on purpose
429Too many requestsTells Googlebot to back off. Kills crawl rate
503Service unavailableWith Retry After header = the correct planned maintenance signal
200 on errorsSoft 404Page says "not found" but returns 200. Common on JS sites, pollutes the index

Core Web Vitals: the current three

The usual fixes

LCP: optimize the hero image and server response time. CLS: reserve space for images, ads, and fonts before they load. INP: break up long JavaScript tasks. Diagnose with lab, verify with field.

Cloudflare features an SEO Lead owns

Cache rules (freshness versus speed), edge redirect rules (faster than server redirects), Workers (inject tags, prerender, programmatic redirects), and bot management (the number one silent Googlebot killer).

0 / 3
Rankings dropped sharply this week with no algorithm update and no site changes from marketing. First place to look:
Sudden cliff drops without content changes are usually infrastructure: bot rules, 5xx spikes, a robots.txt regression. Check what changed at the edge.
Which data determines your actual Core Web Vitals ranking signal?
Google uses field data. Lab data is a simulation for debugging. A perfect Lighthouse score with bad field data still hurts you.
A "page not found" screen that returns HTTP 200 is:
The status code is the machine signal. 200 says "real page here," so Google may index thousands of error screens as content.

Why it paysThe posting lists AEO and GEO by name. When a company writes those acronyms into a $300K job spec, the market has priced the skill. Most SEOs still can't define it. That window is maybe 18 months wide.
Plain EnglishWhen ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, or Google's AI Overviews answer a question, they pick a few paragraphs from across the web to quote. First, you must be findable in normal search, because the AI shops from the same shelves. How retrieval actually works

The technical tactics

Chunk level optimization

Retrieval pulls passages. Every section must stand alone: a heading phrased like the question, the direct answer in the first sentence, detail after. Answer first structure is measurably what gets quoted.

Entity consistency

Identical names and descriptions across your site, LinkedIn, Crunchbase, G2, GitHub, and directories. A product called Estimates on the page and Bids in the URL fragments the entity. You've seen this exact bug on your own site.

Crawler access audit

Check robots.txt and Cloudflare rules for GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot, and Google Extended. Many sites blocked them in the 2023 panic and forgot. Blocked crawler = zero citations from that engine, forever.

Measure it

Run your money queries through the engines monthly and log every citation. Watch referral traffic from chat.openai.com and perplexity.ai. Track branded search volume as the awareness proxy.

Module 7 / The frontier

SEO experimentation: proof over promises

Proving changes cause results instead of asserting it. This is what separates "trust me" SEO from the version that survives a room full of engineers.

Why it pays"This will help rankings" loses to a feature request every time. "We tested on 200 pages and saw a 14% click lift at 95% confidence" wins engineering resources. Experimentation is how SEO buys priority.
Plain EnglishThis module is about proving your change caused the improvement, instead of hoping. You cannot show Google two versions of the same page (that's cloaking), so instead you take 400 similar pages, change 200, leave 200 alone, and watch whether the changed group pulls ahead. The untouched group is your alibi: if traffic rose everywhere because of the season, it rose there too, and comparing the groups subtracts that noise automatically.

Why SEO tests are different

You can't show Google two versions of one page: that's cloaking. So SEO tests split at the page group level. Take 400 similar pages, randomly assign 200 to treatment (new title format, added schema) and 200 to control, and compare each group's trend against its own baseline. The method is difference in differences: did treatment diverge from control after the change?

The statistics, plain terms

Significance

The probability your result isn't random noise. The convention is 95% confidence: a 5% chance you're fooling yourself.

Sample size

Small groups produce noisy results. Underpowered tests that "show" wins are how teams lie to themselves. Knowing your sample is too small, and saying so, is itself the skill.

Measure from recrawl, not deploy

Google has to recrawl treatment pages before the change can take effect. Your Module 2 log skills tell you exactly when that happened. This detail alone separates real practitioners from readers.

The other kind of test: conversion experiments

The posting also asks for A/B and multivariate tests on conversion flows (signup pages, pricing pages) using a platform like Statsig. Different game, same statistics. Here you CAN show two versions to different users, because you're testing on people, not on Google. Vocabulary to own: A/B = two versions of one thing, multivariate = several elements varied at once, and Statsig, Optimizely, and GrowthBook are tools that handle the traffic splitting and the math for you. If you can explain the control group logic above, you already understand these.

Checkpoint

0 / 3
Why can't you A/B test one page's title with Google?
SEO experiments randomize similar PAGES into treatment and control groups, then compare group trends.
Traffic rose 20% on treatment pages after your change. Before claiming victory, you check:
If control rose 18%, your effect is 2 points, not 20. Difference in differences exists exactly for this trap.
Your test window should start:
A change Google hasn't recrawled cannot have an effect yet. Measuring from deploy dilutes real effects with dead time.

International SEO and hreflang

Serving the right language version to the right searchers. Know the failure modes cold; hands on depth can wait until a client needs it.

Why it paysAI companies are expanding internationally right now, so this playbook gets built from scratch at each one. It's in the posting because nobody there has done it yet.
Plain Englishhreflang is a label on each page telling Google "this is the Spanish-for-Mexico version of that English page." One rule generates nearly every failure in the wild: the labels must point both ways. The English page points to the Spanish one AND the Spanish page points back, or Google ignores both. The rest of the module is where the labels live and two habits (forced redirects by location, raw machine translation) that quietly break international sites.

The rule that generates all the failures

Structure decision first

Subdirectories (site.com/mx/) usually beat ccTLDs and subdomains for SaaS: consolidated authority, one infrastructure. Sitemap based hreflang scales best at volume.

Never auto redirect by IP

Googlebot crawls from the US. Auto redirect by location and Google only ever sees your US version. Suggest the local version with a banner instead.

Localize, don't just translate

Currency, pricing, examples, and entities need localizing. Unreviewed machine translation at scale is a quality risk that can drag the whole domain.

Why is IP based auto redirect poison for international SEO?
Force redirect by location and your international pages become invisible to the crawler. Banner suggestions, never forced redirects.

Subdirectories inherit domain authority and share infrastructure. The JS swap option makes alternate languages invisible to crawlers entirely.

Module 11 / Operations

ELT and BigQuery: SEO data at scale

The posting asks for "ELT pipelines" and "BigQuery." Translation: automatically copy all your SEO data into one giant searchable database, because at 80,000 pages the normal tools stop working.

Why it paysSearch Console's interface shows you 1,000 rows at a time and deletes everything older than 16 months. A SaaS site with tens of thousands of docs pages generates data those limits butcher. The SEO who can query the full dataset answers questions everyone else has to guess at.

The jargon, translated

Data warehouse

One big searchable filing cabinet in the cloud where all your data lives together. BigQuery is Google's version. You never install anything or manage a server: you upload data and ask it questions.

Pipeline

An automated job that copies data from point A to point B on a schedule, with no human touching it. "Build a pipeline" just means "make the copying happen every day by itself."

SQL

The language you use to ask a database questions. It reads almost like English ("SELECT url ... WHERE clicks = 0") and an SEO needs about six keywords of it. Taught below.

ELT: Extract, Load, Transform

Extract = pull data out of the source. Load = dump it into the warehouse raw, exactly as it came. Transform = clean and reshape it afterwards, with SQL. Groceries analogy: bring everything home first, cook later.

Why the letters are in that order (ELT vs the older ETL)

The older pattern, ETL, cleaned data BEFORE storing it. If the cleaning logic was wrong, the original data was gone forever. Then cloud storage got absurdly cheap, so the industry flipped: store everything raw, clean it later with a query you can rewrite any time. Postings say ELT because that's the modern default. You don't need to know more than this paragraph.

The pipeline you'd actually build

The magic ingredient for SEOs: Google offers a built in Search Console bulk export. Flip one switch and GSC streams its complete data into BigQuery every day automatically. That's your extract and load solved for free, no code. Your only real work is the T: writing queries.

Why bother: the interface versus the warehouse

QuestionGSC interfaceBulk export → BigQuery
How many rows can I see?~1,000 per reportevery row, millions if they exist
How far back?16 months, then goneforever: you own the copy
Cross it with server logs?impossibleone JOIN query
"Which of 80k docs pages get impressions but zero clicks?"can't answer at scalea six line query

One honest caveat: even the bulk export withholds a slice of rare, privacy filtered queries. Everything else is complete. Also: export starts collecting from the day you enable it, nothing retroactive, so switching it on early is the whole game.

The only SQL you need to start

-- pages people see but never click: your title rewrite list
SELECT url, SUM(impressions) AS impressions, SUM(clicks) AS clicks
FROM searchdata_url_impression
WHERE data_date >= '2026-06-01'
GROUP BY url
HAVING SUM(clicks) = 0 AND SUM(impressions) > 500
ORDER BY impressions DESC

Line by line, in English

SELECT = which columns you want back. FROM = which table to look in (that's the GSC export's page level table). WHERE = keep only rows matching a condition. GROUP BY = collapse many rows into one per URL, adding up the numbers. HAVING = filter on those added up totals. ORDER BY = sort. That's roughly 80% of all the SQL an SEO ever writes: six keywords.

The bill, demystified

BigQuery charges for data scanned per query, not for time or servers, and the free tier covers 1 terabyte of scanning a month. A year of GSC data for most sites is a few gigabytes. Practical habits: SELECT only the columns you need (never SELECT *) and always filter on the date column. Do that and SEO scale work rounds to free.

GSC bulk exportBigQueryLooker StudioScheduled queriesdbt (later)

Looker Studio is Google's free dashboard tool that plugs straight into BigQuery: your queries become charts stakeholders can see. Scheduled queries rerun your SQL daily, which combined with Module 8 is how "automated alerting" gets built. dbt is what data teams use to organize transforms; know the name, learn it only if a role demands it.

The other names in the posting: GA4, Segment, Hex

Google Analytics 4 measures what visitors do AFTER they land: signups, clicks, conversions. Search Console covers before the click, GA4 after, and joining the two in BigQuery is the full picture. Segment is plumbing: it collects events from your site once and forwards them to every tool that needs them, including BigQuery. Hex is the dashboard and notebook tool Anthropic's data teams use to share queries: think Looker Studio's more technical sibling. You don't need to master any of these to interview well. Know what each one is FOR and say it plainly; that already beats most candidates.

Checkpoint

0 / 3
In ELT, where does data cleanup happen?
Load raw first, transform after. The raw copy is permanent; the cleanup is a disposable query. That order is the entire difference from the older ETL pattern.
Why pipe Search Console data into BigQuery at all?
The interface is a summary viewport. At 80,000 pages you need every row, permanent history, and the ability to cross reference logs. Only the warehouse gives you that.
BigQuery's pricing mostly charges you for:
No servers exist for you to pay for. Scanning is the meter, the free tier is 1TB a month, and lean queries keep SEO work effectively free.

Step 4: Questions to drill until they're boring

"Walk me through diagnosing a sudden 40% organic traffic drop."

Strong answer runs the pipeline in order: check for manual actions and algorithm updates first, then infrastructure (robots.txt diff, bot rules, 5xx in logs), then indexing (GSC coverage), then rendering, then SERP feature shifts. The order proves you think in systems. Guessing "content quality" first fails.

"Our docs have 80,000 pages. How do you prioritize?"

Log analysis for crawl allocation, GSC for impression opportunity, internal search data for content gaps, then a segmented plan by template type. Naming data sources before tactics is the senior tell.

"How would you approach AI Overviews for our properties?"

Retrievability first, chunk structure second, entity consistency third, measurement loop fourth. Cite your own citation research. You'll know more about this than most interviewers, and that's the point.

"Tell me about an experiment that failed."

They're testing intellectual honesty. Your title test with its sample size limits is a perfect answer: designed properly, read honestly, learned from. Never claim every test won.

Step 5: Handling the gap question

Someone will ask why a publisher and local SEO background fits a developer tools role. Do not dodge it. The framework: name it before they do, show the overlap is bigger than the gap, and point at receipts. Something like: most of my career was consumer and publisher SEO, which is why over the past months I rebuilt my practice around the technical stack this role needs, then show the log analysis, the watcher, the docs site. A named gap with evidence of closing it beats a pretended qualification every time. Interviewers at this level have seen a thousand fakers and zero honest operators, and the contrast works in your favor.

Step 7: Comp

The posted range is $255K to $320K plus equity. Ranges are posted because regulations require it, and offers usually land below the top unless you bring leverage. Your leverage: published work, the frontier skills (GEO, docs SEO) that thin the candidate pool, and genuine alternatives (your own businesses are a real BATNA, use that calmly). Never negotiate against yourself by naming a number below the posted floor.

Two prerequisites this course can't replace

HTML and CSS. The posting requires enough proficiency to "review code for SEO issues": spot a missing canonical tag, a stray noindex meta, a heading hidden behind JavaScript, a link built as a button. MDN's free HTML and CSS intros plus reading view source on sites you know gets you there in a few weeks of evenings. Tool reps. Screaming Frog, Ahrefs, SEMrush, and Search Console are listed as hands on requirements. Screaming Frog is free up to 500 URLs: crawl your own site weekly during the 90 days until its reports feel boring. Ahrefs and SEMrush both have cheap trial tiers; run their site audits on your own properties so you can speak to what each tool is for.
